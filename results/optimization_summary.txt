Optimization Summary - Both Benchmarks
=======================================

Date : 2026-09-21

A single consolidated view of every optimization in the project: what
was changed, what was predicted, what was measured, and how correctness
was established. The per-step detail lives in the individual
*_comparison.txt files; this is the reference to read first.

All timings use the same pyperf configuration, -p 10 -w 1 -n 5, on the
same QEMU/KVM guest (Ubuntu 22.04.5, Python 3.10.12, pyperf 2.10.0).


================================================================
 HEADLINE
================================================================

    benchmark   baseline   optimized   speedup   reduction
    ---------------------------------------------------------
    raytrace      800 ms      498 ms     1.60x      37.8%
    pyflate      1.12 sec     696 ms     1.61x      37.8%

Both measured end to end in a single session by the reproduction
scripts, not assembled from the individual steps.

Project requirement: >=7% on at least two benchmarks. Both clear it by
more than five times over.


================================================================
 RAYTRACE - 3 OPTIMIZATIONS
================================================================

    #  change                                time    step    cumul.
    ------------------------------------------------------------------
    -  baseline                             799 ms      -        -
    1  hoist loop-invariant shadow ray      622 ms   22.2%    1.29x
    2  remove no-op type assertions         573 ms    7.9%    1.40x
    3  __slots__ on geometry classes        497 ms   13.3%    1.61x

Prediction accuracy

    1  predicted 10-18%   measured 22.2%   underestimate
    2  (no range recorded)  measured  7.9%
    3  predicted  3-7%    measured 13.3%   underestimate

Why #1 beat its prediction: the estimate assumed _lightIsVisible often
returns early on the first occluder, so the redundant Ray construction
would frequently not be paid. The realized 22.2% says the shadow test
more often runs to completion - consistent with a scene where most
visible surface points are lit by at least one of the two lights.

Why #3 beat its prediction: the estimate counted only the attribute
access speedup. It missed that removing the per-instance __dict__ also
removes an allocation per object, across 2,264,715 Vector constructions
per 5 frames. The allocator saving was larger than the lookup saving.

Common theme: NONE of the three optimizations touches the arithmetic.
All three eliminate calls or allocations. This follows directly from
the profile - 3.1 million Python calls per 100x100 frame, with ~10.9%
of perf self time in frame setup and teardown alone.

Correctness

    Raytrace does not verify its own output, so every step was checked
    by rendering a frame from both variants and comparing raw pixels.

        f3c700588a9b6320fb1e46027142f205  raytrace_base.ppm
        f3c700588a9b6320fb1e46027142f205  raytrace_opt.ppm

    Byte-identical, not approximately equal. No change reassociates
    floating-point arithmetic, so any difference would be a bug rather
    than drift. The same hash was independently reproduced on macOS
    and inside the VM.


================================================================
 PYFLATE - 5 OPTIMIZATIONS
================================================================

    #  change                                time    step    cumul.
    ------------------------------------------------------------------
    -  baseline                            1.11 s       -        -
    1  inline the _mask bit-mask helper     1.04 s     6.3%    1.07x
    2  rewrite move_to_front                865 ms   16.8%    1.29x
    3  length-indexed huffman decode        799 ms    7.6%    1.39x
    4  hoist RLE loop length checks         738 ms    7.6%    1.50x
    5  batch literal spans                  690 ms    6.5%    1.61x

Prediction accuracy

    1  predicted  5.9%    measured  6.3%   close
    2  predicted  4-8%    measured 16.8%   large underestimate
    3  predicted 15-25%   measured  7.6%   large overestimate
    4  predicted  3-6%    measured  7.6%   slight underestimate
    5  predicted 5-12%    measured  6.5%   low end of range

The two large misses are the most instructive results in the project.

#2 underestimated because the prediction used move_to_front's own self
time. The slice rebuild creates three temporary lists per call, and the
allocator and garbage collection work that follows is attributed by
cProfile to no single function - it surfaces only in the perf profile,
as list_dealloc and _PyObject_Malloc. The biggest win of the five came
from the change whose cost was the most diffuse.

#3 overestimated because 49% was find_next_symbol's CUMULATIVE time,
which includes the snoopbits and readbits calls it makes. The new
implementation removes the table scan but still performs the bit
reading. The figure that mattered was its SELF time, 15.5%, and the
realized 7.6% sits sensibly below that.

That distinction is exactly why the hardware proposal targets the bit
reader and the symbol decode TOGETHER. In software they are separate
functions with separate costs; in hardware they are one operation.

Correctness

    The benchmark self-verifies: bench_pyflake raises on an MD5
    mismatch, so every timing quoted came from a correct run.

    That is necessary but not sufficient. The bundled data is bzip2,
    which only ever decodes with reversed=False, so the DEFLATE path
    (reversed=True) is never exercised and a wrong answer there would
    pass silently. tests/test_huffman_equivalence.py closes the gap:

        compared 24000 decode attempts across both directions
        no mismatches


================================================================
 CONSIDERED AND DELIBERATELY NOT DONE
================================================================

raytrace
    Vector.normalized calls magnitude() then scale(), paying an extra
    call and an intermediate allocation across 549,435 invocations.
    Sphere.intersectionTime repeats attribute lookups that could be
    bound to locals once.

pyflate
    bwt_transform computes character counts by sorting the whole block
    and then performing 256 bytes.find calls - its own comment calls
    this "semi-inefficient". A 256-bin histogram plus a prefix sum is
    O(n + 256) with no sort. Worth 0.103 s cumulative in the baseline,
    against changes delivering several times that elsewhere.

All three are real. They are recorded rather than implemented because
an optimization that is measured and documented is worth more than one
that is merely applied, and the target was already exceeded.

Regenerate with:  ./script_<name>.sh flamecmp

How they are collected

    perf record -e cpu-clock -F 999 -g  against the stock python3.
    cpu-clock rather than cycles because the QEMU guest exposes no PMU,
    so these measure elapsed time, not retired cycles. See the section
    below on why the stock interpreter rather than python3-dbg.

    Three post-processing steps, all of which preserve sample counts:

    1. tools/collapse_interpreter.py folds each run of CPython call
       plumbing into one [python call] frame, and each run of
       unresolvable frames into one [unresolved] frame. Without this the
       DWARF graphs were 127 rows tall with _PyEval_Vector appearing
       809 times, because reaching one Python call costs a repeating
       cycle of seven C frames.

    2. flamegraph.pl --minwidth drops frames too thin to carry a label;
       they were 92% of the 9,525 boxes and most of the file size.

    3. The threshold is equal in ABSOLUTE time on both sides, not equal
       as a percentage. --minwidth is a percentage of each run's own
       total, and the optimized run is shorter, so a flat 1% was a
       smaller absolute threshold on the optimized side and admitted
       thinner stacks: baseline rendered 40 rows against optimized 73.
       1% of the baseline is now the common threshold and the optimized
       percentage is scaled by the ratio of the totals. This matters for
       the same reason the collection parameters are held identical.

Reading the differential graphs

    Width is time; colour is the change between baseline and optimized.
    BLUE means time was removed, RED means time was added.

    The two profiles are collected with identical parameters - same
    loop count, same sampling frequency - because otherwise the
    difference between them would reflect the collection settings
    rather than the optimizations. This is why the flamecmp stage
    regenerates the baseline instead of reusing an older one.

    The profiles are deliberately NOT normalized. perf samples on a
    timer at fixed frequency, so the totals are proportional to elapsed
    time and the blue area represents real time deleted rather than a
    mere change in shape.

    Some red is expected and is not a defect. The length-indexed
    Huffman decode ADDS dictionary work even as it removes the table
    scan, so parts of the decode path legitimately got more expensive
    while the whole got substantially cheaper.


================================================================
 THE PROFILER USED TO DISAGREE WITH THE BENCHMARK, AND NO LONGER DOES
================================================================

Worth recording in full, because the disagreement was real, the cause
was diagnosed from the profile itself, and acting on the diagnosis
removed it. That last step is what turns a plausible explanation into a
tested one.

The original flame graphs used DWARF unwinding, which needs debug
information, which only python3-dbg carries. Against pyperf they read:

    benchmark   perf, python3-dbg    pyperf, python3
    ---------------------------------------------------------
    pyflate     1.54x - 1.57x        1.61x
    raytrace    1.79x                1.60x

The debug build writes guard bytes around every allocation and
re-validates them on free: the _PyMem_DebugCheckAddress, read_size_t
and write_size_t entries were roughly 12% of the profile. That inflates
any optimization which works by NOT allocating, and two of raytrace's
three changes do exactly that, since __slots__ deletes the per-instance
__dict__ for millions of Vectors and the shadow-ray hoist deletes whole
subtrees of temporaries. pyflate showed a smaller gap because its wins
are more mixed: the length-indexed decode adds dictionary work as it
removes the scan.

That diagnosis makes a testable prediction. Profile the STOCK
interpreter and the gap should close. Frame-pointer unwinding allows
it, because it needs no debug information:

    benchmark   perf, stock python3   pyperf, python3
    -----------------------------------------------------------
    pyflate     5.701 -> 3.595 s      1.61x
                = 1.59x
    raytrace    4.118 -> 2.597 s      1.60x
                = 1.59x

Both agree to within 0.02x. The debug allocator accounted for the
entire discrepancy, so the graphs now measure the same interpreter that
produced the headline timings and their ratios can be quoted directly
rather than discounted as qualitative.

The switch is not free, and the cost is stack quality. Ubuntu compiles
python3 with -fomit-frame-pointer, so mean stack depth was only 5 to 7
frames and some stacks unwound into towers of unresolvable addresses,
now collapsed to a single [unresolved] frame carrying 3.8-4.0% of self
time. The graphs are 4 rows tall against 40 for the DWARF versions:
they rank self time reliably, but no longer show much call hierarchy.
In particular the operator-dispatch chain DWARF exposed
(PyNumber_Subtract -> binary_op -> slot_nb_subtract ->
vectorcall_unbound, the machinery behind Vector.__sub__ being a Python
dunder) is absent from them. That evidence survives in the perf report
self-time rankings and in the cProfile data.


================================================================
 WHAT THE MEASUREMENTS REVEALED BEYOND THE SPEEDUPS
================================================================

1. Raytrace is bottlenecked on CALLING functions, not on the work the
   functions do. 3.1 million Python calls per frame; ~10.9% of perf
   self time in frame allocation and teardown. All three optimizations
   eliminate calls; none improves the arithmetic.

2. Pyflate's Huffman decode share ROSE after being optimized, from
   49.3% to about 50.1%, because the other four optimizations removed
   proportionally more from everything else. Amdahl's law read in
   reverse.

3. Point 2 is what identified the hardware target. The component worth
   accelerating is the one that SURVIVED a serious software effort, not
   the one that merely looked expensive before anyone tried.

4. Point 1 is what ruled raytrace out as a hardware target, despite it
   being the workload that superficially looks ideal for a MAC unit.
   Accelerating arithmetic cannot help a program whose cost is the
   interpretation of that arithmetic.

Both conclusions come from the same pair of profiles. Profiling
answers not only where the time goes but what kind of work it is, and
the second question is the one that determines whether hardware helps.
