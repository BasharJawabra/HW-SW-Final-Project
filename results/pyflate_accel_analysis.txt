Pyflate Hardware Acceleration - Opportunity Analysis
====================================================

Date    : 2026-09-21
Subject : how much of the OPTIMIZED pyflate the Huffman accelerator can
          absorb, and what end-to-end speedup that permits

The question this answers is deliberately narrow. The accelerator is
worth proposing only if the work it removes is a large share of what
remains AFTER the software optimizations, not of the original baseline.
    10|Quoting the baseline's numbers would credit the hardware with work
already eliminated in software.

Per decompression that is 148,271 decoded symbols, identical to the

The share went UP, which is the point
-------------------------------------

find_next_symbol's cumulative share of the run:
    50|
    baseline   1.052 s of 2.132 s   49.3%
    optimized  4.463 s of 7.908 s   56.4%

It is worth being clear about why optimizing something increased its
share. The Huffman rework (length-indexed dict) gained 7.6%. The other
four optimizations - inlined bit masks, move_to_front, the RLE length
hoist, and batching literal spans - together gained considerably more,
and all of them applied to non-Huffman work. Shrinking everything else
    60|made the residue loom larger.

This is Amdahl's law read in the other direction, and it is the honest
argument for the accelerator: Huffman decode is the part of pyflate
that resisted software optimization, and it is now the part that
dominates. A component that stays expensive after a serious software
effort is a better hardware candidate than one that merely looked
expensive before any effort at all.


    70|Correcting for profiler overhead
--------------------------------

56.4% is an overstatement. cProfile charges roughly a fixed cost per
call, and this subtree is unusually call-dense: 6.63 M of the run's
10.81 M calls occur beneath find_next_symbol.

    measured under cProfile     7.908 s   (5 loops)
    real, from pyperf             3.450 s   (5 x 690 ms)
    overhead                      4.458 s over 10,809,699 calls
    80|                                = 0.412 us per call

    subtree calls               6,629,795
    subtree overhead            2.734 s
    subtree time, corrected     4.463 - 2.734 = 1.729 s
    corrected share f           1.729 / 3.450 = 50.1%

So the hardware-replaceable fraction is about 50% of the optimized
runtime, not 56%. The correction assumes cProfile's per-call cost is
uniform across call types, which is approximate: a call into a C
    90|builtin is cheaper to instrument than a Python-level call. The figure
should be read as "about half", and the sensitivity is given below
rather than a single number being defended to three digits.


Ceiling on end-to-end speedup
-----------------------------

With fraction f moved to hardware and the hardware treated as free,
the whole-program speedup is 1/(1-f):

   100|    f = 0.45    1.82x
    f = 0.50    2.00x
    f = 0.56    2.27x

The accelerator is close enough to free for this to be a fair framing.
At the simulated 1.02 cycles per symbol, 148,271 symbols take:

    100 MHz    1.51 ms per decompression
    200 MHz    0.76 ms
    400 MHz    0.38 ms

   110|against roughly 345 ms of software that remains. Even at 100 MHz the
decoder contributes under half a percent of the new runtime, so the
result is bounded by Amdahl's law and not by the hardware's throughput.
Clocking it faster is pointless here; the design is already so far
ahead of the surrounding software that the frequency target is set by
what integrates comfortably, not by what the workload needs.

Expected result: pyflate falls from 690 ms to roughly 350 ms, a ~2.0x
hardware speedup on top of the 1.61x already obtained in software, for
about 3.2x against the 1.11 s original baseline.
   120|

The assumption that would invalidate this
-----------------------------------------

The estimate assumes decoded symbols reach software in bulk. They must
be DMA'd into a buffer that Python then consumes as a single object.

If instead software read one symbol per memory-mapped load, the Python
interpreter would execute ~148,271 read operations per decompression.
   130|At even 1 us per Python-level MMIO access that is ~148 ms, which would
consume most of the 345 ms the accelerator was supposed to save. The
decoder would be fast and the system would not be.

This is the single most important integration constraint in the design
and it is a software-side constraint, not a hardware one. It is why the
proposed interface DMAs a symbol array rather than exposing a
"read next symbol" register.


   140|Why not accelerate more of the pipeline
---------------------------------------

Once Huffman decode is removed, the remaining ~345 ms is dominated by
bwt_reverse, bwt_transform and the MTF stage. Accelerating those too
would raise f, but each addition widens the hardware/software boundary
and increases the data that must cross it, and the returns follow
1/(1-f) which is still gentle in this range: taking f from 0.50 to 0.65
buys 2.00x -> 2.86x.

   150|The stronger reason to stop at Huffman decode is that it is the part
whose cost is intrinsic to sequential bit-serial work, which general
purpose hardware handles badly and dedicated hardware handles well.
The inverse BWT is a large permutation, which is a memory bandwidth
problem rather than a control problem, and a CPU with a cache is not
obviously the wrong machine for it.
